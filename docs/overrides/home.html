A way of keeping notes · for work, home and family

Write it down now.
Decide where it goes later.

Everything goes on today's page as it happens. What matters is posted, word for word, to the note it is about. Once a week you close what is open. Plain text files, done by hand first; a tool and an agent only make it faster.

Try it for a week → Read the method In five minutes

No app, no account. A folder and a text editor are enough, and even a paper notebook works.

Why another method

The problem is rarely writing.
It is deciding where.

Most systems for personal notes fail in one of three places, and anyone learning something meets a fourth. The method answers each with one move.

  1. 01

    Writing stops when it needs a decision.

    Which folder, which notebook, which tag: the question comes during the call, when it is least welcome, every time.

    so Everything goes on today's page. Nothing to decide while you write.
  2. 02

    What is written cannot be found.

    Folders reflect how you thought about something on the day you sorted it. A year later they make sense to nobody.

    so Find things by name and link: both a search and a path to walk.
  3. 03

    What is open slips.

    Tasks and promises stay where they were written. A list that only grows stops being trusted, and is not read.

    so Once a week, every open item gets a decision, where it is.
  4. 04

    What you learn fades.

    And rereading your notes does little to stop it.

    so Write questions, and ask yourself again after a gap.

One week in a work vault

Log, mark, post, find, close.

Every line below is from the example work vault: a project called Apollo, a customer called Acme, two colleagues and a course. All of it is made up; you can browse every file.

  1. LogTuesday, 10:00

    During a call with Acme you write on today's page, with the time, as it happens. No folder, no tag, no form. The page is the inbox, the journal and the record of the day.

    2026-11-10.md 10:00 Call with [[acme]] about [[apollo]] - Their new contact is Marta Kovac
  2. Markas you write

    A sign on an entry says what it is. Four cover most of a day, and they are plain characters you can type anywhere.

    - [ ]
    a task, a promise, something you wait for
    ?
    an open question, at the end of the line
    Decided:
    a decision
    !
    this matters
  3. Postlater, or at the close

    What you will want to find again is copied, word for word, into the note it is about, with a link to its day. The project's decisions can always be checked against the day they were made, and when the two disagree, the day wins.

    The name comes from bookkeeping: in Pacioli's 1494 description, each transaction went first into a day book and was later posted to its account.

    2026-11-10.md - Decided: we cut the mobile app from the first release
    same words
    notes/apollo.md ## Decisions - Decided: we cut the mobile app from the first release ([[2026-11-10]])
  4. Findany time

    You never remember where you put something; you look for its name. A link names the note, not its folder, so moving a note breaks nothing, and every link is a path back: a note's backlinks are its history, day by day.

    lines that link to [[apollo]] 2026-11-09:10 Standup: [[apollo]] demo moved to 2026-11-20 2026-11-10:7 Call with [[acme]] about [[apollo]] 2026-11-11:5 [ ] Budget approval from [[john-roe]] for [[apollo]] (waiting 2026-11-11)
  5. CloseMonday 16, fifteen minutes

    Once a week you read the week and decide on everything still open: a task is done, dated, made someday or dropped; a question or a wait is answered, chased or dropped. Nothing is copied from page to page, and a skipped week costs nothing: the next close finds what is still open.

    • Send John Roe the October invoice, overdue since Fridaydo it today
    • The budget approval, waiting five dayschase John
    • Should we move the demo to a Tuesday?answer it
    • The sprint ended on Sunday: the summary is duedraft it today
    • Tuesday's decision and riskpost to Apollo

The whole vocabulary

Eight words carry the method.

daybook
Your pages, one per day. Every entry goes on today's page first.
entry
One line you write on a page, usually with the time.
mark
A sign on an entry that says what it is: a task, an event, a question, a decision, something that matters.
note
A page about one thing: a person, a project, an area you look after, a topic, a course.
link
A note's name in double brackets, [[apollo]]. The link is the relationship.
post
To copy an entry, word for word, into the note it is about, with a link to its day.
close
The decision on what is open, once a week or on a cycle you choose.
recall
A question you wrote, with the next day to ask yourself again.

One method for all of your life

A release, a renovation and a busy week
use the same pages.

What changes is the vault, not the method. Keep one for work and one shared with your family; each works the same way.

What the research supports, and what it doesn't

Its parts are studied.
The whole is a bet.

Nobody has tested the Daybook Method as a whole, any more than the Bullet Journal or the Zettelkasten. The method makes few claims, and it says how strong each one is.

Whether it fits you

Not the best method.
One that may fit how you think.

It suits you if

  • you lose thoughts while deciding where they go;
  • work, home and family each have their own app and list, and each is one more place to check;
  • you want your notes in plain files you own, readable in any editor for as long as text files are.

Look elsewhere if

  • you need to track a team's work: that belongs on the team's board, and the daybook links to it;
  • you want a visual planner or an art journal;
  • you like to sort as you write. Folders work for some people, and that is fine.

the cost Nothing a day beyond writing, about fifteen minutes a week to close, and nothing at all when you skip.
